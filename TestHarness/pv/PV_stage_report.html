01What was tested, and against what

The model was not modified. The reference is independent of the algorithm.

Where the score comes from

Tracking efficiency is a ratio, and its denominator cannot come from the algorithm being tested. A second copy — pvSweep.slx, same plant, P&O replaced by a fixed duty — is swept across the full duty range at each irradiance. The best steady-state power it reaches is the ceiling: the most this hardware could deliver if the controller were perfect.

Against that ceiling the tracker scores 98.8% to 100% at every irradiance tested. The algorithm is doing its job. Everything below is about the conditions it has been asked to do it in.

02Finding 1 — the DC bus is not regulated

Highest integration risk. Blocks connection to the shared bus.

The boost output feeds a fixed 98 Ω resistor. Nothing controls the bus voltage, so it becomes whatever the duty cycle and the available power make it. Since MPPT moves the duty cycle as a function of irradiance, the bus voltage tracks the weather.

The requirement comes from the other side of the boundary. The DC-link regulation loop in config/harnessParams.m holds the shared bus at 700 V within ±1%; the PV stage is allowed the wider ±5% envelope on the argument that the grid-side converter absorbs the rest. Neither figure is met below full sun.

What this means for integration

This is not a tuning problem, and it is not a defect in the MPPT. It is a missing function: there is no outer loop regulating the output. In the integrated system the bus will be held near 700 V by the grid-side converter, which means the boost stage's duty cycle is constrained by Vpv/Vbus rather than free to be chosen. The MPPT then has to work by commanding current, not by loading a resistor.

Until this stage is re-tested against a stiff 700 V bus, none of its MPPT behaviour here predicts its behaviour in the integrated system. The resistor is doing work in these results that the real bus will not do.

03Finding 2 — P&O stands still during a ramp

Blocks the cloud-transient requirement. Root cause is structural.

Perturb-and-observe decides which way to move by asking whether power went up or down since the last step. While irradiance is falling, power goes down on every step regardless of what the controller did — so the direction test fires every time, and the algorithm reverses on every perturbation.

The consequence is that reacquisition does not start until the disturbance ends. On the ramp the delivered controller needs a further 1.24 s to arrive, against a 0.50 s requirement — but the 1.24 s understates the problem, because the stage also spent the preceding second off the maximum and making no attempt to correct.

This is a documented weakness of fixed-step P&O rather than a mistake in the implementation, which is worth saying plainly: the code is a correct P&O. The standard remedies are to compare against the irradiance trend as well as the power trend, or to move to an algorithm that does not infer direction from power alone — incremental conductance being the usual next step.

04Finding 3 — reacquisition and ripple are one knob

A design trade-off that needs a decision, not a bug.

The perturbation size dD sets both how fast the tracker can chase a change and how much it dithers once it arrives. At the delivered 0.002 per 10 ms the stage slews 0.2 of duty per second, which is too slow for the cloud-edge requirement. Raising it to 0.010 fixes that outright.

It also breaks the ripple requirement. A bigger perturbation is a bigger dither around the peak, and at low irradiance — where the power–duty curve is steep and the duty is close to its rail — that dither reaches 2.5–2.7% against a 2% limit. Neither setting satisfies both requirements.

The decision this forces

A fixed perturbation size cannot meet both specs at once, so the choice is between relaxing one of them and moving to a variable step size — large steps while far from the peak, small steps once arrived. That is the conventional answer and it removes the trade entirely. It is also the same change that would help with Finding 2.

06Suggested order of work

Two of these are the same change.

  1. Replace the 98 Ω load with a controlled 700 V bus, and add whatever regulates the output — most likely a voltage loop commanding current, with MPPT setting the current reference rather than the duty. This is the blocker, and it changes what every other result means.
  2. Move to a variable perturbation step. Large while far from the peak, small once arrived. Removes the reacquire-versus-ripple trade in Finding 3, and shortens the recovery in Finding 2.
  3. Handle changing irradiance explicitly — compare the irradiance trend, not only the power trend, so the tracker stops reversing on every step during a ramp. Incremental conductance is the usual replacement if you would rather change algorithm than patch P&O.
  4. Check the low-duty region near D = 0.19, where power steps 25% for a 0.005 duty change. Worth confirming this is the conduction-mode boundary and not a modelling artefact, since a hill-climber near a cliff behaves badly.

One thing to agree before the next revision

Write down what this subsystem presents at its boundary — signal names, units, sign conventions, sample rates, and the bus voltage it assumes. Mismatched interface assumptions are what actually break integration weeks, far more often than control theory does. This project has already lost a debugging session to a value given in rpm where the connected block expected rad/s. Nothing was wrong with either model.
